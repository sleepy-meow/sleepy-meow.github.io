> **The most useful thing a leader can do for psychological safety is make it demonstrably safe and worthwhile to tell them something they may not want to hear.**
## Most useful things a leader can do
1. **Receive the message well.**
2. **Protect the messenger.**
3. **Do something with the information.**
4. **Explain what happened.**
5. **Then invite more people to speak.**

## Most useful things a leader can do

1. **Respond well when someone speaks up.**  
    The most important moment is the first response to a concern, disagreement, mistake, or request for help. Thank the person, stay curious, and understand what they are seeing before evaluating whether they are right.
    
2. **Protect people from retaliation and reputational harm.**  
    Make sure speaking up does not lead to exclusion, lost opportunities, unusual scrutiny, gossip, or damaging labels. This includes intervening when the retaliation comes from peers or other leaders.
    
3. **Follow through and close the loop.**  
    Explain what will happen next, who owns the issue, and what was ultimately decided. Even when no action is taken, a clear and respectful explanation shows that speaking was worthwhile.
    
4. **Admit mistakes and repair their consequences.**  
    Say when you were wrong, handled something badly, or lacked important information. Repair may also require correcting the record, restoring an opportunity, apologizing publicly, or changing a decision—not merely expressing regret.
    
5. **Invite disagreement before decisions are final.**  
    Ask specific questions such as, “What are we overlooking?” or “What would make this fail?” Make space for less senior and less outspoken people before the most powerful voices shape the discussion.
    
6. **Clarify expectations, priorities, constraints, and decision rights.**  
    People should know what outcome matters, what “good enough” looks like, what is open for discussion, and who will make the final call. When priorities change, say so explicitly rather than judging people against a new standard later.
    
7. **Treat mistakes and bad news as information first.**  
    Begin by understanding what happened, what the person knew at the time, and what conditions contributed to it. Accountability still matters, but learning should come before blame.
    
8. **Apply standards consistently, especially to powerful people.**  
    Address bullying, interruption, contempt, and retaliation even when the person responsible is senior or highly valued. People judge the real culture by what influential people are allowed to do.
    
9. **Lower the practical cost of participating.**  
    Welcome rough ideas, incomplete questions, early drafts, and concerns without ready-made solutions. Use written input, advance materials, thinking time, and smaller discussions so participation does not depend on confidence, speed, or presentation polish.
    
10. **Make safety predictable through consistency.**  
    One good response cannot outweigh a long pattern of defensiveness or punishment. Repeatedly responding with curiosity, fairness, clarity, and follow-through is what turns isolated moments into a trustworthy team norm.

## Most damaging things a leader can do
(pretty much in this order)

1. **Retaliate against people for speaking up.**  
    Excluding someone, removing opportunities, increasing scrutiny, or damaging their reputation after they raise a concern sends the clearest possible message: honesty is dangerous, and everyone who witnesses it learns to stay quiet.
    
2. **Humiliate, ridicule, intimidate, or explode at people.**  
    Public criticism, contempt, yelling, mockery, or hostile questioning makes participation feel personally risky. Even when only one person is targeted, the whole group becomes more cautious.
    
3. **Invite honesty, then use it against people.**  
    Asking for candid feedback and later describing the person as negative, difficult, disloyal, or not a team player turns openness into a trap. It can be more damaging than never asking for feedback at all.
    
4. **Protect favorites and apply standards selectively.**  
    When powerful, senior, or well-liked people are allowed to behave in ways others would be punished for, people learn that the rules depend on status. Trust in both the leader and the process begins to disappear.
    
5. **Blame people for mistakes instead of learning from them.**  
    Looking for someone to punish before understanding what happened encourages people to hide errors and avoid experiments. The organization may appear to have fewer problems simply because nobody reports them.
    
6. **Treat disagreement as a character problem.**  
    Labeling concerns as negativity, poor attitude, lack of commitment, or resistance avoids engaging with what the person is actually saying. People begin editing themselves to appear agreeable rather than useful.
    
7. **Let concerns disappear into a void.**  
    Listening politely but never following up teaches people that speaking is pointless. A leader does not have to agree with every concern, but they should explain what was considered and what happens next.
    
8. **Keep expectations vague and move the goalposts.**  
    Hidden standards, changing priorities, and retrospective criticism make it difficult for people to know what good work looks like. When consequences feel unpredictable, self-protection replaces initiative.
    
9. **Dominate discussions and make participation performative.**  
    Interrupting, dismissing questions, defending every decision, or asking for input only after the decision is made tells people their contribution is not genuinely wanted. Meetings become exercises in reading the leader rather than examining the work.
    
10. **Refuse to admit mistakes or repair harm.**  
    A leader who never says “I was wrong” or corrects the consequences of their actions signals that accountability only flows downward. Unrepaired harm also makes later promises of openness much less believable.
