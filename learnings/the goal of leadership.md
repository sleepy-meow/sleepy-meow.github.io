*"the goal of leadership is to produce more leaders, not followers"*

Why not just followers? Because when organizations grow, they quickly become too complex for any one person to make all the decisions. 

In the past employees in factories where given tasks which they learned to perform rote but they didn’t have to understand why. Management would do the thinking. Today organizations are too complex for management to do all the thinking.

A common misconception is that when a company grows, it will inevitably revert to the factory management practices. It's not, *it just doesn't happen by itself* - for a few reasons:

> "people will just work in the way they have previously done, simply because that's what they know."

A growing company without any processes just leads to chaos. But *if people don't know about all the nice processes* out there, if we don't teach people the skills, they will reach out to the ones they already know (usually traditional management practices). 

Whilst there are certainly some managers out there who enjoy ruling by fear and holding power over people, most managers who act in this way simply don’t know any better. We’re all taught, throughout our lives, via our own experiences, and through movies, books and more, that command-control and using fear as an incentive is an effective way to manage people.

This is a very difficult thing to unlearn. And it takes time – none of us start our careers as already excellent leaders.


This approach is incredibly inefficient, for one simple reason:

An organization is basically a [complex adaptive system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_adaptive_system)
- Agents act on local information, not a global plan
- Order emerges from interaction, not from a controller
- Feedback loops are fast and travel in all directions
- The system self-organizes and reconfigures as conditions change
- Small local changes can cascade into large system-level shifts (nonlinearity)

The intelligence of the system lives in the _network of interactions_, not in any single node.

![[Pasted image 20260703225239.png]]


**A hierarchy is designed to do almost the opposite:**
- Information flows are channeled through defined reporting lines (vertically, mostly)
- Decision rights are concentrated at nodes higher in the tree
- Local agents are expected to act on approved plans, not emergent local information
- Feedback loops are slow and filtered — by the time information travels up, gets decided on, and travels back down, the local conditions that generated it have often changed
- The structure is optimized for control and predictability, not adaptability

The reason it won't work, is you're applying a tool built for stable, separable problems to a domain that is neither stable nor separable.

# the best approach depends on the goal

Innovation is, by definition, messy and unpredictable.

**What happens when you try to run CAS dynamics inside a hierarchy**

A few predictable failure modes show up:

1. **Signal degradation.** Local, high-fidelity information (a frontline employee noticing a problem) gets compressed, reinterpreted, and political filtering gets applied at every layer it passes through. By the time it reaches a decision-maker, it's often unrecognizable or arrives too late to matter.
2. **Latency mismatch.** CAS adaptation requires fast local feedback loops. Hierarchical decision cycles (approvals, alignment meetings, budget cycles) are usually an order of magnitude slower than the environment is changing. The system ends up perpetually adapting to conditions that no longer exist.
3. **Emergent behavior gets pathologized.** If something self-organizes bottom-up that wasn't sanctioned — a workaround, an informal norm, a cross-team collaboration — hierarchies often read this as "going around the process" rather than "the system doing exactly what healthy CAS do." This is a common point of institutional friction: legitimate adaptive behavior looks like insubordination from above.
4. **Local optimization gets punished for not matching global optics.** Agents closest to the problem often make locally rational adaptive moves that look bad in an aggregated report, because the report strips out the context that made the move sensible.
5. **The org tries to compensate with more control, which makes it worse.** The instinctive response to "things feel chaotic" in a hierarchy is to add more process, more approval gates, more reporting — which further slows feedback loops and further discourages local adaptation. *It's a doom loop*: rigidity is applied as the cure for a problem rigidity caused.

**Why this isn't just "bad management" — it's structural**

This is Herbert Simon's and later complexity theorists' point: a hierarchy is a _decomposition_ strategy — it works by breaking a complex problem into modules with minimal cross-dependency, so each module can be managed somewhat independently. That's genuinely useful when the problem _is_ decomposable. But CAS environments are defined by dense, nonlinear cross-dependencies — the opposite of what hierarchical decomposition assumes. You're applying a tool built for stable, separable problems to a domain that is neither stable nor separable.

**How organizations try to reconcile this**

A few real approaches, each with tradeoffs:

- **Loosely coupled hierarchy ("minimal specs")** — set a few non-negotiable constraints and outcomes, leave the _how_ fully to local agents. This preserves some CAS-like adaptability while retaining thin top-level coordination.
- **Sociocracy / advice process (Laloux's self-management model)** — decision rights sit with whoever is closest to the information, with an obligation to seek advice from affected parties and experts, rather than escalate for approval. This tries to keep the CAS property (local, fast decisions) while still coordinating through relationships rather than dictate. https://reinventingorganizationswiki.com/en/theory/self-management/
- **Two-track structures** — a stable hierarchical backbone for genuinely repeatable, low-variance work (payroll, compliance), paired with a networked/self-organizing structure for high-variance, adaptive work (product, R&D). Frederic Laloux's Teal organizations and holacracy attempt versions of this.
- **Sensing infrastructure over control infrastructure** — investing in fast, honest upward information flow (real transparency, psychological safety to surface bad news) rather than more approval layers, because the actual bottleneck is usually signal quality, not decision authority.
