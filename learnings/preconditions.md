> "The only make-or-break factors are the worldview held by the top leadership and by the owners/board."

> [!EXAMPLE] https://reinventingorganizationswiki.com/en/theory/board/

That's it. Two preconditions: the person at the top, and whoever owns the thing. Everything else in the model — self-management, the advice process, conflict resolution, wholeness, salary transparency — is practice, and practice is downstream. Laloux's claim is that you can install any of it and it will not survive if either of those two worldviews isn't genuinely there.

Note what he does not list: middle management, culture, values statements, employee enthusiasm, HR. Those are all explicitly not make-or-break.

The two cautionary cases, because they're the whole argument

BSO/Origin — 10,000 people across 18 countries, entirely self-managing units, minimal HQ. Philips acquired majority ownership in 1996. Traditional management replaced it within months. A founder's account: "My colleagues from Phillips on the board pronounced the word 'unacceptable' regularly and forcefully. In the eyes of Phillips it was 'a deadly sin' to give people the authority to hire personnel or even just give away tickets for a musical."

AES — 40,000 employees, publicly listed in 1991. The board backed decentralization "because they believed it pushed the stock price up, not because it was the 'right' way to operate." Stock went $70 → $5 in the early-2000s downturn. Frightened board imposed traditional oversight, hired a co-CEO; Bakke resigned after nine months, and the whole thing converted back.

> "Fear-driven ownership can abandon Teal practices even when structural decisions weren't the root cause of difficulties."

And the reason the precondition is about worldview rather than support: "Boards may endorse Teal practices during prosperous times, but external pressure can undermine that support quickly. Under current legal frameworks, boards retain ultimate authority."

The contrast cases are all about locking the preconditions in structurally rather than trusting them: Morning Star (Rufer keeps 100% ownership and refuses outside investors), Patagonia (Chouinards own it outright and took B Corp status anyway — "perhaps to protect against future ownership changes"), Sounds True (Simon selected investors for worldview alignment), Buurtzorg (de Blok persuaded uncomfortable board members to resign, and the bylaws state the board does not appoint the CEO).
