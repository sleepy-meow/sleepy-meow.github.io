# conflict avoidance

Avoiding conflict can bring immediate relief. We let something slide, agree before we're ready, or save our concerns for a private conversation with someone else. Sometimes we call this being kind. Sometimes we're afraid of damaging the relationship, looking difficult, or making things worse.

> Most of us avoid clarity because we tell ourselves that we're being kind, when what we're actually doing is being unkind and unfair. Feeding people half-truths or bullshit to make them feel better - which is almost always about making ourselves feel more comfortable - is unkind. (Brene Brown - Dare to Lead)

The concern doesn't go away, though; it just changes form. Other people may read our silence as agreement, while frustration builds around something they haven't had a chance to understand or address. 

This also depends on how people respond. *When disagreement has consequences, avoidance may be a reasonable way to protect yourself.* Making conflict easier requires both practice and repeated evidence that honesty will be treated with care. 

The risk isn't shared equally, either. Whoever has more power carries more of the responsibility for how a disagreement goes, and whoever has less may be too busy calculating the cost to make their case clearly. Having more to lose gets in the way of arguing well.

# normalising conflict

Low-stakes disagreements give us somewhere to practise: making a case, hearing objections, changing our minds, and continuing to get along afterwards.

They can also give us evidence that it's safe to disagree. _I challenged an idea, someone listened, and our relationship was still okay afterwards._ Repeated experiences like this can make disagreement feel less threatening. People watching the exchange get information too: they see what happens when someone offers a different view.

This connects to [[feeling safe and trusted#Predictability|predictability and psychological safety]]. Each disagreement is an opportunity to demonstrate that honesty is welcome. How people respond—especially those with more power—shapes what others will feel able to say next.

The evidence has limits, though. Being able to argue about pineapple on pizza tells you something about the group, but it doesn't establish that questioning a leader's decision is safe. Small, real disagreements about everyday work provide opportunities to build that confidence: suggesting a different approach, questioning an estimate, or pointing out a concern.

Over time, constructive responses can support a useful cycle: feeling safer makes it easier to disagree, and handling disagreement well gives people more reason to feel safe.
# cultural differences

People can have different ideas about what respectful disagreement looks like. For one person, openly challenging an idea signals interest and engagement. For another, the same exchange can feel embarrassing or damaging to the relationship. Erin Meyer explores this in the culture map.

A few differences are worth paying attention to:

- **How directly disagreement is expressed.** “I'm not sure that will be easy” may carry a much stronger objection than the listener realises. Equally, “I disagree” may be intended as an ordinary contribution to the discussion.
    
- **Where and with whom disagreement happens.** Someone may be comfortable challenging a peer privately but reluctant to question a senior person in front of the team.
    
- **How emotion is expressed.** An animated exchange can feel energising to one person and confrontational to another. Volume and expressiveness are imperfect guides to someone's intentions. 
    

These are things to explore with people. Nationality alone won't tell you someone's preferences; family, profession, previous workplaces, and personal experience also shape what feels normal.

It helps to make the team's expectations explicit: how we raise objections, how we invite quieter perspectives, and how we respond when someone challenges a decision. Give people several ways to contribute, including time to think, written input, and smaller conversations.

# we all mess this up sometimes
Sometimes a conversation just ends up being a train wreck. Even when we know what a good conversation looks like, we get defensive, interrupt, make assumptions, or say something sharper than we intended. This is totally normal. What people remember is usually the repair, not the mistake.


> I agree to practice empathy, screw it up, circle back, clean it up, and try again. (Brene Brown, Dare to Lead)

Circling back is a specific move rather than a vague intention: name what you did, resist explaining why it was justified, and ask for another attempt. *“You raised something hard and I got defensive instead of listening. Could we try that again?”* Treating an apology as a skill to practise, backed by different behaviour next time, is more useful than treating it as something you concede once you've run out of arguments.

Be specific about what you did, make room for how it affected the other person, and follow through. You can apologise for how you handled a disagreement while still having a different view on the issue itself.

This connects to [[feeling safe and trusted|psychological safety]]  too. Owning a mistake and repairing it can give people evidence that a difficult moment doesn't have to end the conversation. That evidence becomes stronger when your behaviour changes. An apology followed by the same pattern gives people a different lesson.

We pick up habits from the people around us: how they handle anger, whether questioning someone is welcome, and what happens when we say something uncomfortable. We may have had much more practice keeping the peace than working through a disagreement.

Having a difficult conversation involves several skills at once: noticing your own reactions, explaining what matters to you, listening while feeling challenged, and checking whether your interpretation is right. This takes practice, and discomfort is a normal part of it.





A useful disagreement helps people understand what matters, examine their assumptions, and work out what to do next. It can still feel uncomfortable. The aim is to make room for honesty while keeping people able to listen and respond.

It helps to assume you will be misunderstood at least once, and to treat checking as a normal part of the conversation rather than a sign it's going badly.

> “Communication usually fails, except by accident.” — [Wiio's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiio%27s_laws)

# before a difficult conversation

- **Work out what the disagreement is about.** Is it about the work itself, how decisions are made, or how people have treated each other? These can overlap. A debate about a deadline may also involve broken trust or unclear responsibilities.
- **Know what you want from the conversation.** What needs to be understood, decided, or repaired? “Convince them I'm right” leaves little room to learn anything.
- **Separate what happened from the story you're telling yourself.** What did you actually see or hear? What have you inferred about the other person's intentions? The further you move from observable behaviour towards motive, the easier it is to mistake an interpretation for a fact — see [[clear is kind]].
- **Work out what your part is.** What did you contribute to the situation, including by staying quiet about it until now? It's rare to be entirely outside a disagreement you're in, and arriving with an answer to this makes the rest of the conversation easier to have.

> “You were quiet in that meeting, and the story I'm telling myself is that you were unhappy about the sprint split. How did you see it?”

The invitation only works if you're willing to hear a different explanation.

# when things get heated

1. **Return to the actual question.**  
   “What are we trying to solve here?” Name any shared goal, then make the disagreement specific: “We both want a reliable release. Where we differ is how much risk this change introduces.” Check that the other person recognises that description.

2. **Understand their concern before answering it.**  
   “What worries you most about this?” / “What am I missing?” Summarise what you heard and let them correct you: “You're concerned that the extra scope will push testing out. Have I understood?” Understanding a concern doesn't require agreeing with the conclusion.

3. **Make your own reasoning visible.**  
   Explain what you've noticed, why it matters, and what you're proposing. Leave room for correction: “I think we can include it because the change is small, but I may be underestimating the testing. What would we need to check?”

4. **Separate the emotion from the behaviour.**  
   Strong feeling is not the problem, and treating it as the problem is how concerns get dismissed. Specific behaviour is fair to address: “Being angry is okay. Yelling is not okay.” / “The emotion is okay. Interrupting and put-downs are not okay.” This also works on yourself.

5. **Slow down when listening becomes difficult.**  
   The signs usually show up in your body before they show up in your argument: hot face, tight chest, dry mouth, arms folded. Later, you'll notice you're interrupting, repeating yourself, or rehearsing your reply. Either way, pause. “I'm getting wound up and I'm not listening well. Can we take ten minutes and come back?” Four counts in, hold for four, four out, hold for four, until you can think again. Agree when to return so the pause doesn't become avoidance — and so the ten minutes don't turn into the meeting after the meeting.

6. **Agree on what happens next.**  
   What is settled, what is still disputed, and what would help resolve it? That might be more information, a small experiment, or a decision by the person responsible. Be clear about who will do what and when you'll follow up. See [[how to make decisions]] and [[logging decisions]].

# useful things to remember

**Check the context before judging the person.** The [fundamental attribution error](https://psychsafety.com/the-fundamental-attribution-error/) is the tendency to overemphasise someone's character and underweight their circumstances when explaining their behaviour. “They don't care” is an interpretation to examine.

**Ask what made sense from where they stood.** [Local rationality](https://psychsafety.com/local-rationality/) invites you to consider what someone knew, wanted, and was up against at the time. Understanding that context can help you address the problem; it doesn't erase the effects of their actions.

**Look for common ground, and make room for real differences.** Shared goals can help orient the conversation. They don't make a disagreement about priorities, trade-offs, or fairness disappear.

**Stay curious about emotion.** Ask what is at stake for the person. Don't assume you know why they're angry, or treat their anger as proof that their argument is wrong.

**Listening leaves you free to make up your own mind.** You can take someone's perspective seriously, change your view where it makes sense, and still disagree.

> “I'm brave enough to listen. I don't have to take it all in or add it to my load, but I'm brave enough to listen.”
> — Brené Brown, [*Dare to Lead*](https://brenebrown.com/book/dare-to-lead/)

# what not to do

- **Turn a specific disagreement into a verdict on someone's character.** “You always…” or “You just don't care…” makes it harder to examine what happened.
- **Ask questions with a preferred answer hidden inside them.** “Don't you think that's unreasonable?” is an argument dressed as a question. See [[how to ask the right questions]].
- **Use “calm down” to dismiss the concern.** Address specific behaviour, such as interrupting or insults, while still making room to hear the issue.
- **Treat silence as agreement.** Ask what concerns remain, and give people time or another way to respond.
- **Rush to a compromise before understanding the problem.** Splitting the difference may leave the underlying concern untouched.
- **Take the conversation somewhere else.** Raising it with everyone except the person involved builds a shared account they never get to answer, and it's the habit a scheduled circle-back is there to prevent.

# practise before the stakes are high

Make disagreement part of ordinary work: compare approaches, invite objections, and show that changing your mind is acceptable. A playful debate about pineapple on pizza builds the mechanics — making a case, hearing an objection, giving a point away. Only the everyday response to real disagreement shows whether speaking honestly is welcome.

# example

a teammate comes to you with a concern about someone else.

*what not to do*
- just relay your own interpretation to the other person and leave it at that. Or worse, relay the feedback anonymously so the other person won't even know who to talk to.
- just tell them to talk with them about it and forget the whole thing. There's a reason why they didn't feel comfortable doing it in the first place. They may either lack the skills to do so or not feel safe.
- 

*what to do instead*
ask whether they would feel comfortable bringing it up with them directly, but be prepared for the answer being no. You can offer to do a practice run, give tips on how to structure the conversation, 

talk with both people separately to truly understand each perspective and to find common ground. Then encourage them to have a conversation with each other. if needed, you can offer to join in to the conversation as a mediator. 

*Follow up* 
Make sure the issue has actually been resolved, and that both parties know how the situation was resolved.

*acknowledge the power gradient*
if the other person has more power than the other, then the more powerful person has a higher responsibility to approach the situation with care. the brain of the more powerless person may be overwhelmed with fear and this can get in the way of communicating their needs clearly, as they have more to lose.
