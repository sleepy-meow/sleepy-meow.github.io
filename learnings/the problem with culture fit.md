## culture fit is not a behaviour

_"Not a good culture fit"_ sounds like feedback, but what behaviour is it actually describing?

Culture fit is often treated as if it were an objective quality a person either has or doesn't have. The problem is that unless the culture has been translated into concrete behaviours, _fit_ can mean almost anything: communicates like us, has the same sense of humour, socializes in the same way, doesn't challenge how we do things, feels familiar, or simply makes the evaluator comfortable.

That makes it an especially slippery form of feedback. You can't meaningfully act on _"you aren't a culture fit"_ without knowing what observable behaviour is behind it.

If the concern is _"you repeatedly interrupt people before they've finished speaking,"_ say that.

If the concern is _"you don't share information other people need to do their work,"_ say that.

If the concern is _"you disagree with people more directly than we're used to,"_ then that deserves a different conversation: is there actually a harmful behaviour to change, or is a difference in communication style being mistaken for a problem?

This matters because vague standards leave a lot of room for bias. Dr. Victoria Verlezza describes terms such as _culture fit_, _professionalism_, and _executive presence_ as standards without fixed definitions that can easily become tests of familiarity: does this person resemble the people who already succeed here, and do they make the evaluator comfortable?

That creates a closed loop. If the people who already belong get to define what _fitting in_ looks like, then similarity to the existing group can quietly become evidence of suitability, while difference becomes evidence of poor fit. The organization can then reproduce the culture it already has while believing it is neutrally selecting people who match its values.

The same problem appears in performance feedback. Research summarized by Verlezza shows a broader pattern where some employees receive feedback about skills and outcomes while others receive more personality-based judgments. Once the conversation shifts from _what someone did_ to _what kind of person they seem to be_, bias has much more room to enter.

None of this means culture or values don't matter.

But if a value matters enough to hire, promote, reward, or penalize people for it, it should be possible to describe what that value looks like in behaviour.

_"Not a culture fit"_ is a verdict.

_"Our team relies on people documenting decisions that affect others, and these three decisions weren't documented"_ is feedback.

The useful question isn't _"does this person fit our culture?"_

It's _"what specific behaviour are we evaluating, why does it matter, and would we apply the same standard to someone else?"_
