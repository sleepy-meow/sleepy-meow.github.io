_Practical instructions for leads_

%%
> **The most damaging thing a leader can do for psychological safety is make it demonstrably unsafe and risky to tell them something they may not want to hear.**

The most damaging things, roughly in this order:
1. **Retaliate against people for speaking up.**  
When someone raises a valid concern, make sure to shoot the messenger
2. **Don't protect the messenger.**
3. Humiliate, ridicule, intimidate, or explode at people. 
4. Invite honesty, then use it against people.  
5. Protect favorites and apply standards selectively.  
6. Blame people for mistakes instead of learning from them.  
7. Treat disagreement as a character problem.  
8. Let concerns disappear into a void.  
9. Keep expectations vague and move the goalposts.  
10. Dominate discussions and make participation performative.  
11. Refuse to admit mistakes or repair harm.  
%%

If you want people to stop raising concerns, admitting mistakes, disagreeing with you, or asking for help, these are some of the most effective things you can do.

> **The fastest way to destroy psychological safety is to make it demonstrably costly to tell you something you don't want to hear.**

1. **Shoot the messenger.**  
    Retaliate against someone after they raise a concern, challenge a decision, report a problem, or give uncomfortable feedback. This naturally draws a lot of attention, so people learn what happens to people who speak up.
    
2. **Invite honesty, then use it against people.**  
    Ask people to be candid, vulnerable, or transparent—and later bring what they told you into performance conversations, promotion decisions, gossip, or arguments against them. This teaches people that invitations to openness are traps.
    
3. **Treat disagreement as a character problem.**  
    Instead of engaging with what someone is saying, describe them as negative, difficult, unconstructive, not a team player, or lacking the right attitude. Once disagreement can damage someone's reputation, most people become very good at agreeing. 
    
4. **Humiliate, intimidate, ridicule, or explode at people.**  
    Public embarrassment is especially efficient because you are teaching the whole room at once. Eye-rolling, sarcasm, contempt, raised voices, dismissive jokes, and visible irritation work too.
    
5. **Protect powerful people from consequences.**  
    Apply standards strictly downward and loosely upward. Let high performers, senior people, or favorites interrupt, bully, retaliate, or behave badly without consequence. People will quickly understand that the stated rules are not the real rules.
    
6. **Blame first, understand later.**  
    When something goes wrong, immediately look for the person responsible. Ask "Who did this?" before "What happened?" Make mistakes something people need to defend themselves from rather than information the team can learn from. Soon you will hear about problems only when they can no longer be hidden.
    
7. **Let concerns disappear into a void.**  
    Ask for feedback, then provide no response, ownership, decision, or explanation of what happened next. People do not need to be explicitly punished to stop speaking up; repeatedly discovering that speaking changes nothing works almost as well.
    
8. **Keep expectations vague and move the goalposts.**  
    Leave priorities, responsibilities, decision rights, and definitions of good work unclear. Change them without saying so, then evaluate people against the new standard retrospectively. People will spend their energy trying to predict what you want instead of telling you what they see.
    
9. **Make participation expensive.**  
    Expect people to arrive with polished presentations, complete solutions, perfect wording, strong evidence, and absolute confidence before they are allowed to raise something. Dismiss unfinished thoughts with "come back when you have a proposal." This conveniently removes early warnings and uncertain information.
    
10. **Dominate the room while pretending participation is open.**  
    Speak first, speak longest, interrupt, immediately explain why objections are wrong, and then ask, "Any other thoughts?" People are excellent at detecting when a question is technically open but socially closed.
    
11. **Make people stand alone.**  
    Treat concerns as one person's personal issue even when several people share them. Separate people who compare notes, insist feedback be raised individually, and avoid mechanisms that reveal patterns. A lone dissenter is much easier to dismiss than a shared concern.
    
12. **Keep important decisions mysterious.**  
    Announce outcomes without explaining the constraints, reasoning, trade-offs, or who actually made the decision. When people cannot understand why things happen, they fill the gaps themselves—and usually become more cautious about challenging whatever invisible process produced them.
    
13. **Never admit that you were wrong.**  
    Defend your original decision, reinterpret what happened, quietly change direction, or blame execution. An apology without repairing the consequences works particularly well: it preserves the appearance of accountability while teaching people that nothing materially changes.
    
14. **Reward silence and punish bad news.**  
    Praise people who are "easy to work with," always positive, and never bring inconvenient problems. Become visibly happier when reports are reassuring and irritated when they are not. Soon the information reaching you will become increasingly positive regardless of what is actually happening.
    
15. **Be unpredictable.**  
    Respond warmly to disagreement one day and defensively the next. Psychological safety depends heavily on people being able to predict the interpersonal consequences of speaking. If those consequences depend on your mood, people will choose silence whenever they are uncertain.
    

The important thing is that **you don't have to do all of these**.

A few highly visible incidents can outweigh dozens of invitations to "speak freely." People learn psychological safety primarily from what actually happens after someone takes an interpersonal risk—not from what the company says its culture is.

And because power amplifies behavior, a lead does not need to personally punish everyone. If one person gets burned and everyone else sees it, the lesson scales automatically:

**keep your head down, make the presentation look good, and don't be the person who brings the bad news.**
