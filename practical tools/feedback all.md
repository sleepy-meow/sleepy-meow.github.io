How to deliver feedback and improve psychological safety

If we only remember one thing, we should make it the “Platinum Rule” – Treat others as they want to be treated. That means delivering feedback in a way that the recipient prefers, not the way we prefer to deliver it, or even the way we would prefer to receive it. To do this, we need to ask how they want to receive feedback. Of course, they may not yet feel psychologically safe enough to be honest with us, and may instead tell us what they think you want to hear. But nobody said that this was easy – we must first put the work in to create an environment of psychological safety!

Here are some suggestions for how to effectively deliver feedback that not only increases performance, but also minimises harm. Really well delivered feedback drives improvement, whilst poorly delivered feedback decreases performance and can cause real damage, sometimes lasting for years afterwards.

**Effective Feedback Checklist**
elements of good feedback



**Well intentioned**
This feels like it should go without saying, but when we get the urge to give feedback, it’s important to check in with ourselves and make absolutely sure that our intentions are to help the person improve. Feedback should never be about punishing someone for making a mistake or making them feel bad so that they do it differently next time.

*Feedback that doesn’t come from a place of positive intent isn’t feedback, it’s bullying.*

**Truthful**
When delivering feedback, first recognise that what you think is not “the truth” – we are not an omniscient god :) What happened is our perception, and it’s shaped by our own biases and experiences. We only experience the world through our own rather foggy lens. Before giving feedback, we may want to check in with others, to see whether their perspective aligns with ours. Show humility, and recognise that we could be wrong. Sometimes, recipients may prefer to have a chance to suggest their own improvements first – “How do you think that could have gone better?“. Be aware, however, that this can seem like a “tell me what I’m thinking” exercise, and create anxiety. And in some cases the recipient might not want to tell us an error they’re not sure that we’ve spotted – after all, why give someone a stick to beat you with?

**Non-trivial**
Whilst we might be detail-oriented and want to provide every opportunity for our team to improve, no matter how small, it’s important to recognise that frequent, trivial feedback can have a chilling effect on team members’ efforts. As a result of frequent and seemingly trivial feedback, they may begin to fear that everything they do will be critiqued, and nothing they do is “good enough”. The safest option for them may be to not try at all, or only carry out very low-risk, low-impact tasks where the risk and impact of critique is low. It can be demoralising if we try really hard at something, and still receive critique every time.

**Consensual**
Like sex, or tea, feedback should be consensual. We could ask things like:
    “Are you interested in my observations?”
    “Can I share with you something I noticed?”
    “Are you open to feedback at the moment?”
    “Can we chat about the things we’re both working on improving right now so we can share thoughts with each other about what we’re noticing?”
    “Can we discuss some ideas for improvement?”
    “How do you feel that went”

We’re not always ready for feedback. We might not be in the right mental state for it – we could be too tired, stressed, or distracted, or events in our lives outside of work may be taking up all our headspace.

If we know we didn’t perform as well as hoped on a task, we may be very well aware of any shortcomings and don’t need someone else kicking us when we’re down, however well intentioned that person is. I suspect many of us can think of times when we’ve known only too well that we’ve messed something up, and we already know exactly what to do differently next time. If we give feedback to someone who isn’t ready for it, doesn’t want it, or doesn’t need it, we risk causing more harm than good. 

By delivering feedback badly, or at the wrong time, we risk further damaging someone’s performance and confidence. In the worst cases, they may even simply quit.  Even positively-intended feedback can have the opposite result if delivered poorly. 

**Actionable**
*Feedback must be actionable.* It’s stressful to hear feedback if we don’t know how to act upon it. By actionable, we mean *something we can deliberately do, change, practise, or improve upon.* One way is to try providing actions in the form of experiments, such as “Next time, maybe you could try doing X instead of Y, and see what happens.” By making everything an experiment, the risk of “failing” is significantly reduced.

**Timely**
The further we are in time from an event, the less accurately we remember it, so the best time to learn from something is usually as soon as possible after the event. That said, there are exceptions to this rule. After a particularly emotive or traumatic event we may need time to recover before we can process feedback.

**Specific and Precise.**
*Unspecific or imprecise feedback causes anxiety.* For someone on a driving lesson, hearing “You need to start indicating a few seconds earlier.” is far more useful than “You need to get better at indicating.” Both may be true, but only the former is really useful, while the latter just makes us feel bad and wonder what we’re doing wrong.

**Private**
Unless we’ve previously agreed to provide feedback in public, all constructive criticism should be private. This makes it easier for the recipient to take in and ask clarifying questions without appearing defensive. Public feedback can also damage psychological safety and create misunderstandings for other team members who may not be aware of the context. 

**Delivered from your perspective**
In general, we should not speak for others in delivering feedback. Saying “Bob’s not happy with this project, and says you need to rewrite the presentation deck.” will make the recipient doubt if that’s really what Bob said and wonder why Bob didn’t say so himself. It also shuts down any opportunity for clarification or conversation.

**A two-way conversation**
When giving feedback, make sure there’s time to discuss it in an unhurried way. Ensure the recipient can ask for clarification, or even disagree and reject it. 

When delivering feedback, expect to hear some in return. This may be about the manner in which we provided feedback, or about something else entirely. All the above points still apply of course – it should be actionable, you may ask for clarification, or you may reject it if you feel it’s not true or appropriate, but ensure you’re open to receive feedback in return. Even better, ask for feedback on how you’ve delivered feedback!

**Focused**
Keep feedback to just one or two key points. It can be hard to absorb feedback, especially if there are multiple messages to take in. Once we’re beyond two points, it’s likely we’re going to forget any more, or simply feel overwhelmed and inadequate, hindering our performance further. 

**Not about personality or style**
This is sometimes difficult to discern. Where does personality end and behaviour begin? We can offer practical feedback on technical or operational tasks, but when it comes to how people communicate and interact, it can be harder. We may feel that someone isn’t communicating well, when in fact it’s simply that we have different personalities, styles, or cultures. We can offer to help someone improve their communication, but we must not try to change who they are.

**Combined with positive encouragement**
I sincerely believe that nobody should come away from a feedback conversation feeling dejected. Every feedback conversation should be positive and forward looking, leaving the recipient feeling optimistic and energised. This isn’t easy, but it is important. Imagine if we could get excited about every opportunity to improve ourselves. Imagine if we could consider every feedback conversation with our manager or leader as a chance to get a little upgrade. That’s what feedback should be – an exciting opportunity to do something even better than we did last time.

An extra note for leads:
If you’re a lead, *be aware that almost every comment you make can be perceived as “feedback” by your team members*, even if you don’t intend it as such. What we intend as throwaway comments can have a big impact on people who look up and/or report up to us.
 
When to refrain from giving feedback
Bear in mind that sometimes no feedback might be the better option – if someone needs encouragement, is finding life or work challenging or has recently experienced trauma, the best thing to do may be to simply say “Well done, keep going.” Feedback can wait until they’re in a better place to receive it. This is another reason why it’s critical to check in with the recipient before providing feedback.
 
**Receiving feedback**
As challenging as it can be to give feedback, receiving it can be just as difficult. 

In these cases, remember that since all feedback is based on opinion and perception, *not all feedback is helpful or accurate*. Just because someone has given you feedback, it doesn’t mean you need to act upon it. Their perspective may be skewed, or in the worst cases, their intention may not be positive. So when you’re receiving feedback, consider:
    Is this feedback truthful?
    Is this feedback specific and actionable?
    Is there positive intent behind it?
    Is the person delivering it doing so with care and concern for me?
    Is the person delivering this feedback experienced or qualified to do so?
    Is this the right time and space for me to receive this feedback?

**Appreciate, and Accept or Discard**
If the answer to any of those questions is no, then you have a right to question or even disregard the feedback. *I was once told by a manager to “Be less English”.  I discarded that feedback because I can’t and I don’t want to be less English.*

However, we should also try to resist our natural temptation to defensiveness. Even if some of the answers to the above questions are “no”, there is probably something you can take from the feedback in order to improve or reflect in some way. 

And remember, *feedback is a conversation*, so if you’re giving feedback, expect to receive feedback in return, particularly about how you delivered feedback. We can hardly expect to give feedback to others if we’re not open to it ourselves.

Great feedback can enhance an already high performing, psychologically safe team, but poorly delivered feedback can destroy psychological safety. Be careful, empathetic and mindful when delivering it. Contracting with people about not just how, but when and if, they’d like to receive feedback is powerful, and kind.


# engaged feedback checklist

> [!tip] brene brown - dare to lead / part two - living into our values
#### I know that I’m ready to give feedback when …

1. I’m ready to sit next to you rather than across from you.
2. I’m willing to put the problem in front of us rather than between us (or sliding it toward you).
3. I’m ready to listen, ask questions, and accept that I may not fully understand the issue.
4. I’m ready to acknowledge what you do well instead of picking apart your mistakes.
5. I recognize your strengths and how you can use them to address your challenges.
6. I can hold you accountable without shaming or blaming.
7. I am open to owning my part.
8. I can genuinely thank someone for their efforts rather than criticize them for their failings.
9. I can talk about how resolving these challenges will lead to growth and opportunity.
10. I can model the vulnerability and openness that I expect to see from you.
11. I am aware of power dynamics, implicit bias, and stereotypes.

# the effective feedback model

> [!tip] john amaeichi - the promises of giants / chapter 9

**What is the feedback's intent?**  
If you can't clearly articulate what behavior you're addressing, that's a sign the feedback is suspect and needs more thought.

**Is the feedback contextual?**  
Have you identified the mitigating factors that may be driving the behavior? This is why it matters to know your people. Without a basic understanding of the personal or professional challenges they're facing, you can't know how those challenges are shaping their performance.

**Who is benefiting from the feedback?**  
There should be an obvious answer here. Feedback should benefit the organization, yes—but in the moment of delivery, it must benefit the recipient. Character assassinations aren't feedback. Frustrated outbursts aren't feedback, because the beneficiary isn't the recipient. Before delivering feedback, be sure you can say—with a sound mind—that the recipient is the primary beneficiary, and that none of this is driven by your own needs.

**Is the feedback useful now?**  
When you observe a notable behavior, follow up promptly—but that doesn't always mean delivering feedback on the spot. Some tense moments need to cool first. Still, don't let the moment pass entirely. Feedback loses impact with time and the association between act and consequence weakens.

**Is the feedback real?**  
Do you have an accurate, comprehensive understanding of the behavior you're targeting? Or have you made an assumption, inferred intent, skipped the benefit of the doubt? You may not have the full picture in advance, but feedback should avoid supposition and conjecture as much as possible.

**Is the feedback cruel?**  
You're not obligated to make feedback feel good—critical feedback often doesn't. It can dent the ego and wound. But it should never be cruel, never delivered with harm as the intent. There's a difference between tough feedback that hurts but develops, and feedback meant to leave a permanent scar.

**Is the feedback shared?**  
Would your assessment come as no surprise to others familiar with the situation? Or are you alone in it? An outside opinion is a valuable sanity check—and if others are directly affected, it may be worth consulting them too.
