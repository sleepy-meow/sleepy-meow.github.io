> [!TIP] robert jackall - Moral Mazes: The World of Corporate Managers

One way of looking at "getting ahead in your career" is to see promotions to more fancy titles as milestones or achivements, to get a fancier title, to win the approval of people above you. To upgrade your standard of living so no matter how much you earn, you will need more to maintain it. 

This book can give some sobering insight on what this may look like, and what lies at the end of the ladder (caution: it's a pretty depressing read)

The downside of this is that by the time you reach the next rung, it has already become the new baseline. The promotion does not resolve the need for recognition; it makes the next promotion feel necessary.

More troublingly, the climb can gradually change what you are willing to do to keep climbing. You learn to pay close attention to what people above you want, avoid positions that might become inconvenient later, take credit when things succeed, and distance yourself when they fail. Moral questions are translated into questions of reputation and career risk: not *“is this right?”*, but *“how will this make me look?”*

What lies at the end of the ladder is not necessarily freedom. The higher your salary, status and standard of living become, the more costly it can feel to disagree, step down or leave. You may gain more authority while becoming more dependent on the institution—and on the approval of the people whose expectations brought you there.

This is not an argument that promotions or ambition are inherently bad. It is an argument against letting an organizational hierarchy become your main measure of progress. A career can also be judged by whether you are gaining autonomy, developing useful skills, doing work you consider meaningful, and preserving your ability to say no or walk away.

# confronting our freedom 

Where _Moral Mazes_ examines how organizations encourage people to surrender their moral independence, _Confronting Our Freedom_ asks what it would mean to reclaim it.

> [!TIP] peter block & peter koestenbaum - confronting our freedom

Jackall focuses on the pressures surrounding the individual. Corporate managers learn that getting ahead depends on reading what powerful people want, adapting themselves accordingly and avoiding responsibility for outcomes that could damage their careers. The organization may formally give them authority while informally rewarding conformity.

Block and Koestenbaum focus on the freedom that remains within those pressures. Even when none of the available choices are attractive, saying *“I had no choice”* can conceal the fact that we chose security, approval or belonging over the alternatives. Their argument is that genuine accountability cannot simply be imposed from above: it begins when people recognize their own capacity to choose.

Read together, the books create a useful tension.

_Moral Mazes_ prevents _Confronting Our Freedom_ from becoming naïve or victim-blaming. People make choices, but they do not make them under equal conditions. Refusing, dissenting or leaving may carry serious financial and social consequences—especially after your identity and standard of living have become dependent on the organization.

_Confronting Our Freedom_ prevents _Moral Mazes_ from becoming fatalistic. Institutional incentives may explain why people compromise their values, but they do not make those compromises inevitable or remove personal responsibility for them.

Freedom in this sense does not mean having unlimited options or being able to fix the organization by yourself. It means recognizing the choices that remain and deciding consciously what you are—and are not—willing to exchange for another step up the ladder.

Seen this way, career success might mean more than accumulating status. It might also mean preserving your capacity to disagree, refuse, leave and remain someone whose values are not determined entirely by the institution employing you.
