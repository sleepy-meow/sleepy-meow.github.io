So a few weeks ago I got myself a boat, and quickly discovered the difference between being a crew member, and a captain. 

Sailing is a good example of where hierarchy makes sense, for practical reasons. But it has less to do with being in control, but about responsibility. That ultimately, you are responsible for making sure everyone on board makes it safely back home. 

The thing that crossed my mind wasn't 'bwahaha finally i can be the one to tell others what to do', but to start learning about what safety equipment to buy, what skills to learn to not end up in a situation I wouldn't be able to get out of, either because of the boat, the skills of others on board, weather conditions, or my own incompetence to handle those conditions.

And at this point I was slowly becoming aware of my own incompetence. 

> for someone who is used to being in control, to being in a situation they don't fully understand, they can often be blind to their own ignorance. (turn that ship around)

Having sailed quite a bit when I was younger, mainly on dinghies, and not for the past 15 years, I recognized the urge to appear like I had a better idea what I was doing than I did. I hesitated asking questions that I wasn't sure anymore whether they were obvious or not, whether they would make me look like a fool. But the sea doesn't give a shit. It doesn't treat you more kindly just because you were uncomfortable to ask silly questions. 

> _"the goal of leadership is to produce more leaders, not followers"_

Relying on just one person knowing what to do in case of an emergency would be foolish. A captain may become incapacitated or fall overboard, so others need to know what to do (ideally starting from fishing your ass out of the water). 

Shit rarely hits the fan because of one single mistake, but a cascade of mistakes: noticing something needs to be fixed but thinking "i'll just fix this tomorrow", "i'll just practice the whole man overboard situation later", "i won't need an emergency beacon since i won't be going that far out and can just use my phone", "sharing my route plan with someone seems overkill, why bother anyone with it", "the weather forecast looks fine", etc.

> This ship will never sink


# taking calculated risks

There's probably nothing more humbling than getting caught up in waves that are a bit too big than you're comfortable with, realizing that all your life decisions up until this point will decide your fate. Where all you can do is hold on to something, and think to yourself that the ship is designed to handle the beating it's currently taking, and that you've prepared in case it won't.

# analogy to leadership at the workplace
It's easy to adapt a mindset of responsibility when the consequences are immediate danger of drowning. It's easier to ignore when sitting comfortably in an office, especially when you don't see the impact your decisions might have on others. But that doesn't change the fact that the most important job of a lead is to care for the wellbeing of the team.
