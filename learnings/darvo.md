**DARVO** stands for **D**eny, **A**ttack, **R**everse **V**ictim and **O**ffender. It's a defensive strategy some people fall into when they're confronted about something they did wrong: rather than engage with the accountability, they flip the conversation so that *they* become the wronged party and the person raising the concern becomes the aggressor.

The term was coined by **Jennifer J. Freyd, PhD** (University of Oregon; founder of the Center for Institutional Courage) in a 1997 paper on betrayal trauma. As she defines it, DARVO occurs "when an actually guilty perpetrator assumes the role of 'falsely accused' and attacks the accuser's credibility and blames the accuser of being the perpetrator of a false accusation." The offender "rapidly creates the impression that the abuser is the wronged one, while the victim or concerned observer is the offender."

---

## The three moves

**D — Deny.** The wrongdoing didn't happen, wasn't a big deal, or wasn't their fault.
- "That never happened."
- "You're remembering it incorrectly."
- "It wasn't that big of a deal."
- "You're blowing it out of proportion."

**A — Attack.** Go after the credibility, sanity, motives, or character of the person raising it.
- "You're acting crazy / delusional."
- "You're a liar."
- "You're just trying to manipulate me / make me look bad."
- "No one would believe you anyway."

**RVO — Reverse Victim and Offender.** Flip the roles: *I'm* the real victim, *you're* the one hurting *me*.
- "I'm the real victim here."
- "Why are you punishing me?"
- "You should be apologizing to me."
- "You treated me worse than I ever treated you."

The signature is all three **stacked together, right after someone is held accountable**. Any one phrase on its own can be innocent — defensiveness is human. DARVO is the *cluster*, not the one-liner.

---

## How to recognize it

Look for these in combination:

1. **Simultaneous denial *and* attack** — not "let me understand," but "that's false AND you're a liar."
2. **Credibility attacks** on the person raising the concern (crazy, unstable, manipulative, attention-seeking).
3. **Rapid role reversal** — the accused repositions as the wronged party within the same conversation.
4. **Manufactured confusion** about who actually got hurt.

A useful tell: after the conversation, the person who raised a legitimate concern walks away feeling **guilt, shame, self-blame, or confusion**. That feeling is the *intended effect* of DARVO — it is not evidence that they were wrong.

---

## Institutional DARVO

The same pattern at the level of an organization: when a harm is reported, the institution denies it happened, attacks the reporter's motives or stability, and frames itself as the aggrieved party. In a workplace it looks like a company that, on receiving a complaint, questions the reporter's record and casts the organization as the real victim of a "disgruntled" employee.

---

## Important caveat

DARVO is a **red flag, not proof of guilt.** Innocent people can feel defensive too, and a single denial isn't a verdict. What the research does establish is that exposure to the full pattern measurably harms the person doing the confronting — it shifts both them and any observers toward less belief and more blame. Knowing the pattern exists is itself the antidote: it's hard to be moved by a tactic you can name as it happens.

---

## The contrast: what accountability actually sounds like

The opposite of DARVO isn't silence — it's ownership. Genuine accountability leans on statements like:

- "I take responsibility for what happened."
- "I realize what I did was wrong."
- "I understand how much this hurt you."
- "I'm sorry. There is no excuse for what I did."

Worth keeping in front of you for two reasons:

1. **When you're confronting someone** and the reply is a stack of Deny / Attack / Reverse — the confusion or self-blame you feel afterward is the *effect* of the tactic, not a sign you were wrong to speak.
2. **When you've been called out** — scan your own reaction. If you reach for Deny / Attack / Reverse, that's the moment to pause and pick from the accountability set instead. That's the whole move from defensiveness to ownership.
