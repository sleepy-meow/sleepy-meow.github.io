**What is the feedback's intent?**  
Just as clear objectives make the most of a meeting, they make the most of feedback. If you can clearly articulate what behavior you're addressing, it's more likely to land as substantive. But if your rationale is muddled—if you can't quite explain what you're correcting—that's a sign the feedback is suspect and needs more thought.

**Is the feedback contextual?**  
Have you identified the mitigating factors that may be driving the behavior? This is why it matters to know your people. Without a basic understanding of the personal or professional challenges they're facing, you can't know how those challenges are shaping their performance.

**Who is benefiting from the feedback?**  
There should be an obvious answer here. Feedback should benefit the organization, yes—but in the moment of delivery, it must benefit the recipient. Character assassinations aren't feedback. Ad hominem attacks aren't feedback. Frustrated outbursts aren't feedback, because the beneficiary isn't the recipient. And beware your own state of mind: as anxiety rises, metacognition declines. At a 9 out of 10 stress level, you might believe you're only at a 4 or 5. So be vigilant. Before delivering feedback, be sure you can say—with a sound mind—that the recipient is the primary beneficiary, and that none of this is driven by your own needs.

**Is the feedback useful now?**  
When you observe a notable behavior, follow up promptly—but that doesn't always mean delivering feedback on the spot. Some tense moments need to cool first. Still, don't let the moment pass entirely; keep notes for each of your reports. And don't wait too long, either. Feedback loses impact with time, like rubbing a puppy's nose in a stain long after the deed—the association between act and consequence weakens.

**Is the feedback real?**  
Do you have an accurate, comprehensive understanding of the behavior you're targeting? Or have you made an assumption, inferred intent, skipped the benefit of the doubt? You may not have the full picture in advance, but feedback should avoid supposition and conjecture as much as possible.

**Is the feedback cruel?**  
You're not obligated to make feedback feel good—critical feedback often doesn't. It can dent the ego and wound. But it should never be cruel, never delivered with harm as the intent. Think of rugby versus boxing: in rugby you can get a concussion, but injuring the brain isn't the aim. In boxing, the knockout _is_ the goal. There's a difference between tough feedback that hurts but develops, and feedback meant to leave a permanent scar.

**Is the feedback shared?**  
Would your assessment come as no surprise to others familiar with the situation? Or are you alone in it? An outside opinion is a valuable sanity check—and if others are directly affected, it may be worth consulting them too.
