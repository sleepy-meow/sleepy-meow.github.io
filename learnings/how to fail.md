Finding a good idea often means trying things that don’t work. Sometimes those attempts help us refine an idea; sometimes they reveal something we weren’t looking for.

We might not even entirely know what we are looking for in advance, but stumble upon an invention through failing at something else.

Before deciding how to respond to a failure, we need to understand what kind it was. In the book *The Right Kind of Wrong*, amy edmondson defines three categories of failures:

1. **Preventable:** deviation from a known good procedure in routine work. Never good news. Fix with training and system design.
2. **Complex:**  a novel confluence of factors in a familiar system, but not preventable in the conventional sense. Respond by analyzing the system, not the person.
3. **Intelligent:**  Useful when it tests a worthwhile idea and gives us information we can act on


# Make it safer to experiment
Reducing avoidable failures can give us more room to try new things. When a small mistake is less likely to cause serious harm, we can spend less energy worrying about what might go wrong and more on exploring what might work.

One approach is to layer safeguards. The Swiss cheese model illustrates this: every layer has gaps, but another layer can catch what slips through. The layers reduce the chance that a mistake turns into a larger failure.

We can also make mistakes easier to recover from. An undo button, for example, lets us experiment without losing our work.

The aim is to provide enough protection for people to explore, without making experimentation unnecessarily difficult.

Failing well means being thoughtful about what we try, limiting the potential downside, and using what we learn to decide what to do next.
